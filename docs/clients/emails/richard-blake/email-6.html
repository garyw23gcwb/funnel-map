When someone you love dies, the world knows what to do, more or less.

There are rituals. For a while, you're the person who has lost someone, and other people adjust.

The grief has a container.

When a living child decides you no longer exist, none of that happens.

There is no ceremony. No community shows up.

And the story the culture has agreed on says your child did something brave. If you are grieving, you are grieving the consequences of your own behaviour.

Your pain is read as evidence of what you did, rather than what you have lost.

There is a name for this kind of grief. Pauline Boss called it ambiguous loss: someone physically absent but psychologically present.

She was writing about dementia, and soldiers missing in action. It fits estrangement with particular precision.

Your child is alive. Living a life that goes on without you, sometimes in plain sight online.

You cannot mourn fully because you are still hoping. You cannot stop hoping because your child is still alive.

If that's where you've been, you are not confused. That is what this kind of loss does to people.

My course gives this a whole stage of its own: the anger, the grief, and giving this loss a name.
